Teacher’s Reference: Musical Assumptions & Design Trade-offs

A concise overview of the harmonic model, minor-key conventions, voice-leading rules, and pedagogical simplifications built into this trainer.

1. Why are chords restricted to plain triads with roots in the bass (no V7, no bass inversions, no cadential 6/4)?
  • Why plain V instead of V7: When V7 is used among triads, students quickly bypass functional listening by simply hunting for a 4-note chord or the 4–7 tritone. Keeping all seven chords as pure 3-note triads (I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi, vii° in major; i, ii°, III, iv, V, VI, vii° in minor) forces the ear to judge harmonic gravity and bass relationship rather than chord density.
  • Why roots in the bass: The left hand plays scale degrees 1 through 7 in the baritone register so bass recognition maps 1:1 to the Roman numeral in Stage 2. Cadential I6/4 is excluded here because its dominant bass (5) conflicts with its tonic upper tones (1, 3).
2. Which minor scale is used for minor keys (natural, harmonic, or melodic)?
Rather than forcing a single theoretical scale across all degrees, the trainer uses the standard classical composite functional minor taught in conservatory harmony:
  • Harmonic minor for Dominant function (V, vii°): Scale degree 7 is raised (♯7) to create the leading tone, giving V a major triad quality and vii° a diminished triad quality so dominant resolution to i is unmistakably directional.
  • Natural minor for Mediant (III): The mediant triad uses the unraised ♭7 (III major, the relative major triad) rather than the harsh harmonic-minor augmented triad (III+), which rarely functions as a simple diatonic mediant in foundational ear training.
  • Subdominant & Tonic triads (i, ii°, iv, VI): Built with the natural ♭6 scale degree, yielding the characteristic minor tonic (i), minor subdominant (iv), half-diminished-prep triad (ii°), and warm major submediant (VI).
3. How are chords voiced, and how does the engine handle part-writing in major and minor?
4-part close keyboard style (1 bass note in the left hand + 3-note close triad within one hand's span in the right hand), rather than open SATB chorale spacing:
  • Why close keyboard position: Wide SATB spacing encourages the ear to track individual contrapuntal lines. Tight right-hand voicings (G3–C5, span ≤ 9–12 semitones) fuse psychoacoustically into a single harmonic color while keeping a clear acoustic window above the bass (E2–E3).
  • Part-writing constraints: Common tones are held in the exact same voice; moving voices step to the nearest chord tone. When the bass steps by 2nd (e.g., IV → V, iv → V, or V → VI), the right hand moves in contrary motion to prevent parallel 5ths and parallel octaves with the bass. On authentic D → T resolutions, the leading tone (7) resolves up by semitone to 1.
  • No augmented seconds in minor: In progressions moving between ♭6 and ♯7 (such as iv → V, VI → V, or V → VI), the voice-leading engine strictly forbids any upper voice from leaping across the 3-semitone augmented second (♭6 ↔ ♯7).
Trade-off on vii° and ii°: In classical repertoire, vii° and ii° normally appear in first inversion (vii°6, ii°6) to soften the diminished fifth above the bass. Here, both retain their root in the bass so that every chord preserves a consistent 1:1 root-in-bass mapping when students transition to Stage 2.
4. How are the seven chords grouped into the three functional families in Stage 1?
Stage 1 uses Hugo Riemann's three-function taxonomy across both modes:
  • Tonic (T): I, vi, iii (Major) / i, VI, III (Minor) — Centers of rest, tonic prolongation, and deceptive resolution (sharing two common tones with the tonic).
  • Subdominant (S): IV, ii (Major) / iv, ii° (Minor) — Pre-dominant departure (both contain scale degree 4).
  • Dominant (D): V, vii° (Major & Minor) — Active tension containing the leading tone (7 / ♯7) demanding resolution.
Contextual nuance on iii / III: While the mediant can behave as a dominant preparation in some repertoire, in this trainer's syntactic corpus it is used in its tonic-prolongation and descending-step / circle-of-fifths roles (I–iii–IV, i–III–iv, i–III–VI–V, i–VI–III–iv).
5. How do 3-chord and 4-chord progressions work, and will students hear unidiomatic syntax like V → IV?
Students can choose 3 Chords or 4 Chords alongside two progression generators in the header:
  • Musical Patterns & Substitutions (Default): Draws from curated classical 3-chord and 4-chord syntaxes covering full functional cycles (T–S–D–T, e.g., I–IV–V–I, i–iv–V–i, I–vi–ii–V, i–VI–ii°–V), authentic/half cadences (ii–V–I, ii°–V–i), deceptive resolutions (I–IV–V–vi, i–iv–V–VI), and tonic prolongations. Unidiomatic retrogressions (V → IV, vii° → ii) are excluded.
  • Random Diatonic Triads: Draws 3 or 4 diatonic chords without immediate repeats. Useful once an advanced student starts "gaming" Syntactic Mode by deducing the final chord via theory rules rather than listening.
6. Why is the Tonic Anchor a single I / i chord rather than a full I–IV–V–I cadence?
  • Same-key drilling efficiency: When practicing 10–20 progressions in the same key, hearing a 4-chord cadence before every question causes ear fatigue and doubles exercise time. A single grounded root-position tonic triad re-confirms home quickly.
  • Tip for Random Key mode: When switching keys or using Random Key, students should press A (Tonic Anchor) an extra time to let the new major or minor tonic settle before judging the progression.
7. Why synthesized sound instead of recorded acoustic piano samples?
  • Preventing "Soprano Tracking": Acoustic piano samples have bright hammer transients around 2–4 kHz, making it easy for students to simply sing the top soprano melody note and deduce the chord mathematically.
  • Controlled Harmonic Blend: The synthesizer uses decaying harmonic partials (zero organ-like sustain plateau) with softened inner/soprano velocities (0.52–0.55), a grounded bass (0.66), and a 25ms legato overlap so the four voices fuse into a unified harmonic color.
