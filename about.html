Teacher’s Reference: Musical Assumptions & Design Trade-offs

A concise overview of the harmonic model, voice-leading rules, and pedagogical simplifications built into this trainer.

1. Why are chords restricted to plain triads with roots in the bass (no V7, no bass inversions, no cadential 6/4)?
  • Why plain V instead of V7: When V7 is used among triads, students quickly bypass functional listening by simply hunting for a 4-note chord or the 4–7 tritone. Keeping all seven chords as pure 3-note triads (I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi, vii°) forces the ear to judge harmonic gravity and bass relationship rather than chord density.
  • Why roots in the bass: The left hand plays scale degrees 1 through 7 in the baritone register so bass recognition maps 1:1 to the Roman numeral in Stage 2. Cadential I6/4 is excluded here because its dominant bass (5) conflicts with its tonic upper tones (1, 3).
2. How are chords voiced, and how does the engine handle part-writing?
4-part close keyboard style (1 bass note in the left hand + 3-note close triad within one hand's span in the right hand), rather than open SATB chorale spacing:
  • Why close keyboard position: Wide SATB spacing encourages the ear to track individual contrapuntal lines. Tight right-hand voicings (G3–C5, span ≤ 9–12 semitones) fuse psychoacoustically into a single harmonic color while keeping a clear acoustic window above the bass (E2–E3).
  • Part-writing constraints: Common tones are held in the exact same voice; moving voices step to the nearest chord tone. When the bass steps by 2nd (e.g., IV → V or V → vi), the right hand moves in contrary motion to prevent parallel 5ths and parallel octaves with the bass. On authentic D → I resolutions, the leading tone (7) resolves up by semitone to 1.
Trade-off on vii°: In classical repertoire, vii° normally appears in first inversion (vii°6, scale degree 2 in the bass) to avoid doubling the leading tone. Here, vii° keeps scale degree 7 in the bass so that every chord preserves a consistent root-in-bass mapping when students transition to Stage 2.
3. Why are vi and iii grouped under the Tonic (T) family?
Stage 1 uses Hugo Riemann's three-function taxonomy:
  • Tonic (T): I, vi, iii — Centers of rest and tonic prolongation (sharing two common tones with I).
  • Subdominant (S): IV, ii — Pre-dominant departure (both contain scale degree 4).
  • Dominant (D): V, vii° — Active tension (both contain the leading tone, 7).
Contextual nuance on iii: While the mediant (iii) can behave as a dominant preparation in some repertoire, in this trainer's syntactic corpus it is used strictly in its tonic-prolongation / descending-step role (I–iii–IV, I–iii–vi, iii–vi–ii).
4. Why is the Tonic Anchor a single I chord rather than a full I–IV–V–I cadence?
  • Same-key drilling efficiency: When practicing 10–20 progressions in the same key (e.g., C Major), hearing a 4-chord cadence before every 3-chord question causes ear fatigue and doubles exercise time. A single grounded root-position I chord re-confirms home quickly.
  • Tip for Random Key mode: A single major triad in isolation cannot clear a previous key's dominant from short-term memory. When switching keys or using Random Key, students should press A (Tonic Anchor) an extra time to let the new tonic settle.
5. How are progressions chosen? Will the student hear unidiomatic syntax like V → IV?
The student can switch between two generators in the header:
  • Musical Patterns & Substitutions (Default): Draws from 24 classical 3-chord syntaxes covering authentic/half cadences (ii–V–I, I–IV–V), deceptive resolutions (IV–V–vi, V–vi–IV), tonic prolongations (I–vi–ii, I–iii–IV), and dominant openings (V–I–IV). Unidiomatic retrogressions (V → IV, vii° → ii) are excluded.
  • Random Diatonic Triads: Draws any 3 diatonic chords without immediate repeats. Useful once an advanced student starts "gaming" Syntactic Mode by deducing the 3rd chord via theory rules rather than listening.
6. Why synthesized sound instead of recorded acoustic piano samples?
  • Preventing "Soprano Tracking": Acoustic piano samples have bright hammer transients around 2–4 kHz, making it easy for students to simply sing the top soprano melody note and deduce the chord mathematically.
  • Controlled Harmonic Blend: The synthesizer uses decaying harmonic partials (zero organ-like sustain plateau) with softened inner/soprano velocities (0.52–0.55), a grounded bass (0.66), and a 25ms legato overlap so the four voices fuse into a unified harmonic color.
